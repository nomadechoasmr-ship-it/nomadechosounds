Where our rain is recorded

There are two places, and you can hear the difference:

  • A window at home in Almaty. The black-screen rain tracks are recorded at an open window in the city, at night, with the recorder on the windowsill. That is where the "rain on window" and "rain on pavement at 4 AM" sounds come from.
  • Under a roof in the Tian Shan foothills. The rain tracks with video are recorded in the mountains above Almaty, with the recorder sheltered under a roof so it stays dry and the rain lands around it.

Backstage: recording rain in the mountains.

The recorder is the same Zoom H5 with a fur windscreen that we use for water. Our full field setup is in how we record water sounds. What we measured in our window rain

We measured the raw take behind Rain on Window Sounds for Sleeping · Real Night Rain at 4 AM: steady night rain with no thunder, recorded on a Zoom H5 on the windowsill of an open window at home in Almaty, at about 4 AM, as WAV 48 kHz / 24-bit. We analysed a 15-minute section (19:20–34:20) of the 37:53 take, in 400 ms windows with both channels pooled: the same definitions as our water measurements.

Lower is steadier. Rain: median window level −35.91 dBFS, 2,250 windows. The "short ticks" row uses the highest sample in each window instead of its average level, so it catches knocks too short to move the level; the brook has none above 10 dB (0.5% of windows above 6 dB). Brook, river and birdsong figures are from our measurement data.

Steadier than birdsong, less steady than our water. The rain's loudness variation of 1.51 dB is less than half of the dawn chorus (3.73 dB), but about five times the brook (0.31 dB) and more than twice the river (0.62 dB). If you need the most even sound, for headphones or for tinnitus, the Mountain Brook is still the steadiest thing we have recorded.

The rain itself does not change; single drops do. The loud windows are heavy drops hitting the window ledge, not swells in the rain: 36 of 2,250 windows (1.6%) were more than 6 dB above the median level, and 14 (0.6%) more than 10 dB above it. The loudest drops reached full scale (0 dBFS), and 2.7% of windows held a knock whose peak stood more than 10 dB above the typical peak: about four a minute. They are short, low knocks (63–250 Hz) on top of an even rain, and they are what a light sleeper is most likely to notice.

A midrange hush, not a hiss. Measured in octave bands, the energy peaks between 500 Hz and 2 kHz. Above 2 kHz it falls by about 3 dB per octave, and from 8 to 16 kHz by another 6.5 dB. That broad hump in the middle is neither white nor pink noise, and it is why this rain sounds softer than a bright white noise.

Wide in headphones. The left/right correlation (Pearson) over the whole section was +0.50, against +0.59 for the Mountain Brook measured the same way, so the two channels share only part of their sound and the rain spreads around you instead of sitting in the middle.

From the raw take to the video

These numbers describe the recording, not the audio level of the video. For the video, Andrey raised the take by 11.5 dB, used a limiter to hold down the heaviest drops, removed one 42-second stretch and looped the result, which brings it to about −18.9 LUFS. No compressor and no generated sound were added.

Thunder or no thunder?

Steady rain is a fairly even wash of sound. Thunder adds something different: rolls that rise out of the rain and fade again. Some people find that rumble cosy; for others, every roll pulls attention back to the sound.

  • If you are a light sleeper or sudden changes in sound tend to wake you, start with steady rain, no thunder.
  • If you like a storm atmosphere and sleep through it, rain with distant thunder is the softer choice; heavy rain with thunder is the most dramatic.
  • If you use sound because of tinnitus, Tinnitus UK suggests that most people do best with a sound that "doesn't demand too much attention and is pleasant to listen to" [1]. A steady rain without thunder fits that description better than a storm.

Our measured window rain above shows that even rain without thunder has sudden sounds: a few heavy drops on the ledge, in 0.6% of windows more than 10 dB above the rain. Why steadiness matters at night is explained on the measurement data page.

Timer or all night?

Both work; it depends on why you play the sound. Tinnitus UK notes that bedside sound devices often have a timer "to switch off once you are asleep", and that sound left on at a low volume can be a soothing distraction from tinnitus if you wake in the early hours [1]. The NHS advises people with tinnitus not to sit in total silence, and lists soft music or sounds as a way to be distracted from it [2].

A short track or a timer suits falling asleep; a long track left on quietly suits waking in the night. Volume, speakers versus headphones and timers are covered in more detail in how to use a masking sound at night. Whatever you choose, keep the volume comfortable.
