Best natural sounds for tinnitus, ranked by our measurements (2026)

November 2026 · 5 min read

Short answer: of the natural sounds we have recorded and measured, a mountain brook ranks first, a mountain river second and dawn birdsong last. The brook varied in loudness by only 0.31 dB with no sudden peaks, the river by 0.62 dB, and the birdsong by 3.73 dB with 42 loud events per minute. For masking, the steadier the sound, the fewer gaps it leaves for the ringing.

Most "best sounds for tinnitus" lists are opinions. This one is a ranking of real recordings, made by Andrey in the Tian Shan near Almaty, and measured the same way before any of them became a long track. We only rank what we have measured, so the list is short. It will grow as we measure more.

Loudness variation is the standard deviation of the level in 400 ms windows. A sudden peak is a window more than 6 dB above the median level. Lower is steadier in every column. All numbers, the method and a CSV are on our measurement data page.

How we ranked them

A masking sound works in the background. If its loudness keeps moving, your attention follows the movement, and in the quiet moments the ringing comes back. So we ranked on steadiness, using three measures in this order:

  • Loudness variation: how much the level moves from moment to moment.
  • Sudden peaks: splashes, knocks or calls that jump out of the sound.
  • Tone centre: whether the "pitch" of the whole sound holds still or jumps around.

We did not rank on how pleasant a sound is. That is personal, and it matters, which is why the last section of this page is about choosing between the top two.

1. Mountain Brook: the steadiest

The brook is the most even sound we have measured. Its loudness moved by 0.31 dB, nearly all moments sat within about 1 dB of each other, and not a single window rose more than 6 dB above the flow. Over the analysed excerpt its level drifted by only 0.2 dB, and its tone centre stayed between 1115 and 1136 Hz.

It almost did not happen. In photos the spot looked like a splashy cascade, and Andrey nearly rejected it. In practice the rocks break the water into fine foam, which sounds like an even hiss with no impacts. Since then we judge a stream by measuring it, not by how it looks.

Its one flaw was a lean to the left: the left channel was 3.5 dB louder than the right. In headphones over a night you would feel that, so we fixed the balance before looping. Full numbers and the preparation chain are in the Mountain Brook recording notes.

2. Mountain River: louder, still constant

The river is rougher and heavier than the brook, but it does not surprise you. Over 20 measured minutes its loudness varied by 0.62 dB, sudden peaks above 6 dB made up 0.031% of the time, and there were none above 10 dB. The level drifted by 0.6 dB, and the tone centre sat a little higher, at 1142–1182 Hz, so its rush sounds slightly brighter.

It was also the easier recording to prepare. The channels were already within half a decibel of each other, and the take was recorded at a good level, so the final track needed only +2.1 dB of gain (Mountain River recording notes).

3. Dawn birdsong: last, and why

Birdsong is the sound people most often expect to see at the top of a list like this. In our measurements it came last on every count. Its loudness varied by 3.73 dB, twelve times more than the brook. The gap between its quiet and loud moments was 12.83 dB. It had 42 loud events per minute, a new call about every 1.4 seconds, and its tone centre jumped between about 2,200 and 4,200 Hz.

For tinnitus that pattern is a problem twice over. Each call is a change the ear notices, and between the calls there are quiet gaps where the ringing comes straight back. Birdsong can still be a pleasant short wind-down sound on a timer. It is not a sound to mask through. The full analysis is in is birdsong good for sleep?

What the research says

Two small studies point the same way as our numbers, and both are worth reading with their limits in mind.

  • A pilot study of 21 people in the Journal of the American Academy of Audiology compared white noise with several commercially available custom sounds. All of the sounds reduced tinnitus annoyance compared with tinnitus alone, and two of them, a water sound and a nature sound, were judged significantly more effective than the others (Henry et al., 2004).
  • A preliminary study of 10 adult patients at a tinnitus clinic in Malaysia compared white noise, ocean waves and an amplitude-modulated tone. White noise gave the best suppression, with an effect in every participant, but the difference between the three sounds was not statistically significant. Most patients chose ocean waves for sound therapy (Wan Mohamad et al., 2018).

Neither study tested our recordings, and neither shows that any sound treats tinnitus. What they suggest is that natural water sounds can be as useful as plain noise and are often preferred, and a sound you like is a sound you keep using.

Not ranked yet

We also publish lake waves from the Kapchagay shore, rain and a slow marsh stream. They are not in this ranking because we have not yet measured them in the same way. When they are measured, they will be added to the data page and to this list. Our noise tracks are not ranked either. They are not generated: each one is shaped from Andrey's own water recordings.

Choosing between brook and river

Both top recordings are steady enough for masking. The choice comes down to what sits well with your ringing and your room:

  • The brook is softer and the most even. It suits quiet rooms and headphones, where any change is easier to notice.
  • The river has more weight and a slightly brighter rush. If the brook feels too soft against your ringing or the noise outside, try the river.

Keep the volume comfortable. What tinnitus clinics advise about volume, speakers, headphones and timers is covered in our guide on how to use a masking sound at night, and the wider picture is in the tinnitus masking guide. For sleep rather than masking, see brook vs river vs birdsong for sleep.
