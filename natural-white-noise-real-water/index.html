Natural white noise made from real water, not generated

November 2026 · 5 min read

Short answer: yes, white noise can be natural. Every colour-noise track on our channel (white, pink, brown, grey, green and blue) is shaped from Andrey's own recordings of real water in the Tian Shan near Almaty, never from a noise generator. The water is only equalised, filtered and softened at its peaks, so the natural texture of the stream stays in the sound.

A noise generator makes a perfectly even, random signal from nothing. We do the opposite: we start with a long take of a mountain stream, and of water running through an old steel pipe on a riverbank, and change only the balance of low and high frequencies until the water sounds like the colour we want. This page explains where the water comes from, how it is shaped, and why we never put a compressor on it.

Where the water comes from

The raw material is always one of two kinds of recording, both made by Andrey in the Tian Shan mountains above Almaty:

  • Mountain streams. Long takes of even, foamy water, recorded with a Zoom H5 a little back from the stream so that single splashes blend into one layer. How we choose and record these spots is in how we record water sounds.
  • The old pipe. On one riverbank, half sunk into the slope and overgrown with moss, there is an old steel pipe left from Soviet-era construction. Water runs through it and feeds its own tiny stream, and its sound is one of the sources our noise tracks are shaped from.

The story of how the pipe was found is in our Tian Shan field notes.

How water becomes white, pink or brown noise

A noise colour is simply a balance between low and high frequencies. White noise sounds bright and hissy, pink noise is softer at the top, and brown noise is deep and rumbling, with most of its weight in the low end. Grey, green and blue are other balances in between or above.

Running water already sounds close to noise: thousands of tiny bubbles and splashes, none of them repeating in a pattern. So we do not add anything to it. In an editor, Andrey shapes the recording in three ways:

  1. Equalising: turning some frequency ranges up or down until the water leans toward the colour we want.
  2. Filtering: removing the parts of the spectrum that do not belong in that colour, for example the bright top for a deep brown noise.
  3. Taming peaks: softening the few moments that stand out, such as a louder splash, so nothing jumps out during the night.

The settings change from recording to recording, because no two streams sound the same. What does not change is the source: real water, recorded on location.

Neither kind is "better" for everyone. Some people prefer the clean, uniform hiss of a generator; others find it harsh and like the softer, living texture of water. If you have tried generated white noise and did not like it, a noise made from water is worth a try. If you prefer the water to sound like water, our unprocessed recordings, such as the river sounds for sleep, keep the full natural character.

Why we never use a compressor

A compressor makes quiet parts of a recording louder and loud parts quieter. On music that can be useful. On a nature recording it does harm: it lifts the background noise and the recorder's own hiss along with the water, and it flattens the natural dynamics that make water sound real.

So our processing is limited to trimming, channel balance, a gentle high shelf and normalisation. That only works if the take is recorded at a good level in the first place. Our Mountain River is an example: its loudest moment reached −6.2 dBFS and its average sat at −30.3 dBFS, so the finished track needed only +2.1 dB of gain. Nothing had to be pushed up, and no hiss came up with it.

Every water take we publish as a measured recording is checked for steadiness, sudden peaks and tone drift; the numbers are on our nature sound measurement data page. The noise tracks themselves have not been measured yet.
