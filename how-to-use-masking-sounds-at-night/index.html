How to use a masking sound at night

October 2026 · By Andrey · 5 min read

Short answer: play a gentle, steady sound a little quieter than your tinnitus, so you can still hear both, through a speaker or soft headphones, and either set a timer or let it run quietly until morning. That is what the tinnitus clinic leaflets we read recommend. The sound itself should not change much: our steadiest water recording varies by only 0.31 dB, birdsong by 3.73 dB.

I'm Andrey, a field recordist from Almaty. I record the mountain water behind our tracks and measure every take before it becomes a night track. I am not an audiologist, so the advice on volume and use below comes from the clinic and charity sources listed at the end. What I can add is what we check in the sound itself.

1. Volume: a little below the ringing

This is the part people most often get wrong. The natural reflex is to turn the sound up until the ringing disappears. The sources we read advise against that as a habit:

  • Tinnitus UK suggests using sound "at a level that is a little quieter than your tinnitus most of the time". A loud noise that drowns the tinnitus out can give short-term relief, but "does nothing to encourage long-term habituation".
  • An NHS audiology leaflet from Berkshire Healthcare describes the same point: set the sound slightly lower than your tinnitus, so "you should be able to hear both sounds at the same time", enough to blend with it but not drown it out.
  • Leeds Teaching Hospitals puts it simply: a level "where you can just hear it in the background".

A practical way to find that level: start with the sound off, then turn it up slowly until the ringing begins to blend into the water, and stop just before it vanishes. Set it once while you are calm, not in the middle of a bad night.

2. Speaker, pillow speaker or headphones

The leaflets mention pillow speakers, sound pillows and soft headbands with built-in earphones, mainly so a partner does not have to listen too. Which one you choose is a matter of comfort. What changes is how much a flaw in the recording matters.

In headphones, left and right must be equally loud. Our Mountain Brook take came out of the recorder 3.5 dB louder in the left channel. On a speaker across the room you might not notice. In headphones over a night it feels like the water is pulling to one side, so we corrected it before making the track. The Mountain River was only 0.46 dB off and needed just a small correction.

3. A timer, or all night

Both approaches appear in the sources. Tinnitus UK notes that bedside sound devices often have a timer that switches off once you are asleep, and also that sound left on at a low volume gives "a soothing distraction" if you wake early. The Berkshire NHS leaflet suggests starting the sound about 30 minutes before bed and letting it play at a low volume until morning, which can help you drift back to sleep.

If you wake up when the sound stops, a timer is probably not for you. If silence at 3 a.m. is when the ringing comes back, leaving the sound on quietly is the option the leaflets describe.

For a sound that runs all night, the recording has to hold up for hours. Two things we check before publishing a long track:

  • Drift. The level must not creep up or down over time. Mountain River drifted only 0.6 dB RMS over 20 measured minutes.
  • The loop seam. A long track repeats one long take. If the join has a jump in level, you hear a small "event" every time it comes round. The Mountain Brook seam measured 0.00 dB.

4. A sound that does not demand attention

Leeds Teaching Hospitals advises that the sound be "gentle" and "not change too much", and recommends against radio or TV at night because they keep the mind active. The Berkshire leaflet says neutral nature sounds are usually better for falling asleep. Tinnitus UK says most people do best with a sound that "doesn't demand too much attention" and is pleasant to listen to.

5. Keep it simple and repeatable

Pick one sound you like, one volume and one way of playing it, and use it the same way each night for a while before judging it. Changing the track every night makes it hard to know what helps. Tinnitus UK also notes that many people find sound useful while tinnitus is intrusive and need it less as they get used to the tinnitus, so it is normal for your routine to change over months.

FAQ

How loud should a masking sound be at night?

Tinnitus UK and NHS audiology leaflets suggest a level a little quieter than your tinnitus, so you can hear both sounds at once. Drowning the ringing out completely can bring short-term relief, but the leaflets say it does not help your brain learn to ignore the tinnitus.

Is a speaker or headphones better for sleep sounds?

The clinic leaflets we read mention pillow speakers, sound pillows and soft headbands with built-in earphones, mainly so a partner does not have to hear the sound. In headphones, both channels of a recording should be equally loud: our Mountain Brook take leaned 3.5 dB to the left before we fixed it.

Should I use a timer or leave the sound on all night?

Both are used. Tinnitus UK notes that sound devices often have a timer to switch off once you are asleep, and also that sound left on at a low volume can help if you wake early. An NHS leaflet suggests starting the sound about 30 minutes before bed and letting it play quietly until morning.

What kind of sound works best at night?

A gentle sound that does not change much and does not demand attention. NHS audiology advice says neutral nature sounds are usually better for falling asleep and advises against radio or TV at night. In our measurements a mountain brook varied by 0.31 dB, while dawn birdsong varied by 3.73 dB with 42 loud events a minute.

When should I see a doctor about tinnitus?

The NHS says to see a GP if tinnitus is regular or constant, getting worse, or affecting your sleep or concentration, and to ask for an urgent appointment if it beats in time with your pulse. Tinnitus after a head injury, or with sudden hearing loss, facial weakness or vertigo, needs emergency care.
