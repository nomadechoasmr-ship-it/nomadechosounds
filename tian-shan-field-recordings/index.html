Tian Shan field recordings: mountain water from the Ile Alatau

Updated October 2026 · 6 min read

The water sounds on this site and on our YouTube channel come from one mountain range: the Ile Alatau, also called the Trans-Ili or Zailiysky Alatau. It is the northernmost range of the Tian Shan, and the city of Almaty sits right at its foot. This page explains where and how we record.

The range

The Ile Alatau runs east to west for roughly 300 kilometres along the southern edge of Almaty. Its highest point, Talgar Peak, rises to about 4,973 m. Above the city there are hundreds of small glaciers, and their meltwater feeds the streams and rivers that cut down through the forested foothills.

We record around 1,500 m, in the foothill belt where the valleys narrow and the slopes are covered with spruce and broadleaf trees. At this height the water has already gathered into steady streams, but the valleys are still close enough to reach in a day trip.

Why spring

In spring, melting snow from higher up keeps the streams full and moving. A full stream breaks over rocks into a dense, even hiss. In late summer and autumn the same streams drop, and the sound becomes thinner and more uneven, with individual drips and splashes that are harder to sleep to.

How we pick a spot

Most of the work happens before the recorder starts. We look for three things:

  • No traffic. Roads follow most valleys, so we walk far enough upstream that engine noise disappears. We record on weekdays, when the trails are empty.
  • Even water. A stream that breaks into fine foam sounds steady. A single waterfall or a stone that knocks in the current adds impacts that ruin a sleep track.
  • Distance from the water. Too close and the recorder hears every splash. A few metres back, the sound blends into one smooth layer.

We also learned not to judge a spot by how it looks. One stream that looked like a noisy cascade turned out to be the steadiest recording we have. Every recording is measured before we use it, and some, like dawn birdsong, do not pass.

Autumn in the Big Almaty gorge

In autumn we followed the river up the gorge of the Bolshaya Almatinka, the Big Almaty river, to a spot at 1,466 m, where we recorded several river takes on the Zoom H5 and filmed the water. The water here runs fast and very clear over pink and grey granite, and in October the poplars along the banks turn yellow while their leaves collect between the stones in the current.

Where the river widens it runs shallow over a bed of small rounded pebbles, and the surface breaks into fine ripples and foam instead of single splashes. That is the kind of water we look for: a lot of small, even sound and no single loud impact.

The road to the water

One of the roads to our recording spots: up the Alma-Arasan gorge in the Ile Alatau, the northern range of the Tian Shan, climbing to about 1,800 m. Listen to the engine. This is why we walk far upstream from the road before recording.

Recording setup

Audio is recorded on a Zoom H5. We aim for a level high enough to keep the recorder's own hiss far below the sound of the water. Each take runs about 20 minutes or more, so a long sleep track needs as few loop repeats as possible. Video is filmed separately on an iPhone 16 Pro in 4K for our visual channel.

Two more setups from the gorges: the recorder on a granite boulder a little back from a fast stream, and on a ledge above the river under a rock wall, with the phone filming beside it.

The secret pipe

One of our favourite places was found by accident. During a long take by a mountain river, Andrey, the sound recordist behind Nomad Echo, stepped away so his footsteps would not reach the recorder, and started walking along the bank.

A little way upstream, half buried in the slope, he found an old steel pipe left from Soviet-era construction work on the river. Over the decades it has sunk into the ground and grown over with moss, and water now runs through it into its own tiny stream. Few people have ever seen it.

In early summer the moss around it comes into bloom, and the water falls through it in thin, glassy threads. We call the sound "crystal tears of a mossy spring". We came back to film the drops in macro while the Zoom H5 recorded beside them in the moss.

A few frames from that session: the old pipe itself with the recorder in the grass below its drip, and the thin threads of water falling over the moss.

Water from this pipe is also one of the sources we shape into our noise tracks.

Waves at Kapchagay

Our lake and shore sounds come from the Kapchagay reservoir near the town of Konaev in Almaty Region, recorded in spring 2026. On calm days the water only laps at the shore, which gives the soft, even wave sound we use for sleep tracks. We record on weekdays, when the beaches are empty.
