How steady are nature sounds? Our measurement data

Updated 26 October 2026 · 5 min read

Short answer: steady flowing water is far more even than birdsong. Our Mountain Brook recording varied in loudness by only 0.31 dB and had no sudden peaks at all. A dawn birdsong recording varied by 3.73 dB, twelve times more, with 42 loud events every minute. For sleep and tinnitus masking, where any change in the sound pulls your attention back, that difference is the whole story.

We record every sound on this site ourselves, outdoors, and we measure each recording before it becomes a long track. This page collects all of those numbers in one table, explains how they were measured, and lets you download them as a CSV. It grows as we measure new recordings.

What each number means

  • Loudness variation is the standard deviation of the level, measured in 400 ms windows. It answers one question: how much does the sound move from moment to moment? A third of a decibel is a tiny movement. Several decibels is a sound that keeps changing.
  • Spread (P95–P5) is the gap between the loud end (95th percentile) and the quiet end (5th percentile) of those windows. A large spread means quiet gaps followed by loud moments. For tinnitus, the gaps matter as much as the peaks, because the ringing comes back in them.
  • Sudden peaks are windows more than 6 dB (or 10 dB) above the median level: a splash, a knock, a bird call. Water that is broken into fine foam produces almost none.
  • Tone centre is the spectral centroid, roughly the “pitch” of the whole sound. A narrow range means the character of the sound holds still. Birdsong sat between about 2,200 and 4,200 Hz, the range human hearing is most sensitive to, and kept jumping inside it.
  • Drift is how far the level wanders over the whole measured section. It catches a stream that slowly gets louder as snowmelt rises.
  • Energy below 40 Hz is the share of energy in the deep rumble range, where wind and distant traffic show up. Near zero means a clean recording.
  • Left / right balance matters in headphones. The brook leaned 3.5 dB to the left, which you would feel over a night, so we corrected it before looping.

How we measured

The water recordings were made on a Zoom H5 as WAV files at 48 kHz / 24-bit, outdoors, on weekdays when the valleys are quiet. Spoken slates at the start and end of each take are left out of the analysed section. The flaws the numbers reveal, like the brook's channel imbalance, are what we then fix in preparation.

We use the numbers to decide what becomes a long sleep or masking track. There is no single pass mark. A recording is used when it has low loudness variation, no real sudden peaks, a stable tone and clean low end. Anything that keeps changing, like birdsong, is rejected for all-night use however pleasant it sounds.

Limits of this data

  • Each sound is one recording at one place and time. Another brook, or the same brook in a different month, will measure differently.
  • The analysed sections differ in length: 85 seconds for the brook, 20 minutes for the river, 2 minutes for the birdsong. Longer sections are more representative.
  • These are acoustic measurements, not a clinical test. They show which sounds are steady, not how any one person's tinnitus will respond. The research behind steady sounds for masking is summarised in our tinnitus masking guide.
