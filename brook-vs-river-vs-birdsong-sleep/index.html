Brook vs river vs birdsong for sleep: a measured comparison (2026)

November 2026 · 6 min read

Short answer: for sleeping through the night, choose the brook if you want the quietest, most even sound, and the river if you need more weight against noise outside. In our measurements the brook varied in loudness by 0.31 dB with no sudden peaks, the river by 0.62 dB with peaks above 6 dB only 0.031% of the time. Dawn birdsong varied by 3.73 dB with 42 loud events a minute, so keep it for a short wind-down on a timer.

All three are real recordings. The brook and the river were recorded by Andrey in the Tian Shan near Almaty in 2026 and measured before they became long tracks. The birdsong is a dawn recording we measured the same way and then rejected for all-night use. This page puts the three side by side and turns the numbers into a practical choice. It is listening advice, not medical advice.

What the numbers mean for a night

A sleep sound plays for hours while you are not paying attention to it. What matters is not how nice it sounds in the first minute, but whether anything in it changes enough to be noticed later. Three rows of the table answer that.

  • Loudness variation and spread. The brook barely moves: almost every moment sits within about 1 dB of every other. The river moves twice as much, but 2 dB is still small. Birdsong has a gap of nearly 13 dB between its quiet and loud moments, which means silence, then a call, then silence again.
  • Sudden peaks. The brook had none. The river had a tiny share of windows above 6 dB and none above 10 dB. Birdsong had 42 loud events a minute, a new one about every 1.4 seconds.
  • Tone centre. Both waters sit near 1,100–1,200 Hz and hold still. Birdsong jumps between about 2,200 and 4,200 Hz, a bright range that keeps changing.

Advice from tinnitus clinics on night-time sound points the same way. Leeds Teaching Hospitals advises making sure a night sound "is gentle and does not change too much", and recommends against radio or TV at night because they are likely to keep your mind active. Tinnitus UK says most people get on best with a sound that "doesn't demand too much attention". Measured that way, the brook and the river qualify. Birdsong does not.

Brook or river: which to choose when

Both waters are steady enough to run all night. The choice depends on how you listen and what else is in your room.

In headphones

Headphones put every flaw of a recording right at your ears, so the most even sound is the safest bet. That is the brook. Its one flaw was a lean to the left: straight from the recorder, the left channel was 3.5 dB louder than the right. Over a night in headphones that feels like the water pulling to one side, so we corrected it before looping. The river was only 0.46 dB off from the start and needed just a small correction.

On a speaker

A speaker across the room softens small differences, and the room adds its own sound. Here the river is often the better start: it is rougher and heavier than the brook, so if the brook feels too soft against the noise outside, try the river before you turn anything up. If you share a bed, Tinnitus UK notes that a pillow speaker or sound pillow makes the sound less audible to a partner.

Light sleeper or heavy sleeper

If small sounds wake you, take the brook. It is the only recording we have measured with no window at all above 6 dB of its median level. If you sleep through almost anything and simply want a fuller, steady rush, take the river: it moves a little more than the brook, but no window in 20 measured minutes rose 10 dB above its median level. Either way, start with the volume low. Leeds Teaching Hospitals suggests a level "where you can just hear it in the background".

Where birdsong fits

Birdsong is the most cheerful of the three and the worst for sleeping through. It also has a hidden cost: birds are small and far from the microphone, so our dawn recording needed about 35 dB of gain to reach a normal level, and that lifted recorder hiss and distant traffic with it. The river, recorded at a good level, needed only +2.1 dB.

If birds help you relax, use them at the start of the night only. Play a short birdsong session while you wind down and let it stop by itself. Tinnitus UK mentions that bedside sound devices often have a timer that switches off once you are asleep. Then let a steady water sound, or silence, take over. The full birdsong analysis is in is birdsong good for sleep?

Limits of this comparison

  • Each sound is one recording at one place and time. Another brook or river will measure differently.
  • The analysed sections differ: 85 seconds for the brook, 20 minutes for the river, 2 minutes for the birdsong.
  • These are acoustic measurements of the sound, not a sleep study. They show which sounds are steady, not how you will sleep with them. Try one sound for several nights before you judge it.
