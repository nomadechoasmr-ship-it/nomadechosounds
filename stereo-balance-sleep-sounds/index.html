Stereo balance in sleep sounds: why 3.5 dB matters in headphones

November 2026 · By Andrey · 4 min read

Short answer: in headphones each channel goes straight into one ear, so if one channel is louder, the whole sound sits to that side for as long as you listen. Our Mountain Brook recording had its left channel 3.5 dB louder than the right; on a speaker you would hardly notice, but over a night in headphones it would lean to the left. We measure balance on every take and corrected this one with a pan filter before looping.

What we found in the brook

Mountain Brook is the steadiest recording we have: 0.31 dB of loudness variation and no sudden peaks more than 6 dB above its level. It was recorded on a Zoom H5 (WAV, 48 kHz / 24-bit) in the Tian Shan mountains near Almaty on 20 April 2026. By every steadiness measure it passed. The one thing the numbers caught was balance.

Balance is 20·log10(RMS of the left channel ÷ RMS of the right channel) over the whole measured section; a plus sign means the left side is louder. Correlation is the Pearson coefficient between the two channels over the whole section (0.587 in the rerun, shown as 0.58 on the recording page). “—” means not reported. All numbers are from the raw takes, before any processing.

The rerun with our own measuring script confirmed the first result to within about a tenth of a decibel, on both the analysed section and the whole part we used for the track. So this was not a fluke of one excerpt: the left side was louder for the entire take.

Why a few decibels matter in headphones

One of the cues your hearing uses to tell where a sound comes from is the difference in level between the two ears: a sound off to one side reaches the nearer ear louder, because the head shadows the far ear. With headphones that cue is created directly. Whatever level difference is in the file becomes a level difference between your ears, and the sound is heard toward the louder side [1].

For music that is a creative choice. For a sleep or masking track it is a flaw. The point of a steady water sound is that nothing about it asks for attention, and a sound that sits off-centre all night is one more thing you notice. With the brook, the lean would hardly be noticed on speakers, where both ears hear both channels. In headphones, over a whole night, the sound would sit noticeably to the left.

We do not claim a 3.5 dB lean does anything to your ears. It is simply an error in the recording that headphones make obvious, and it costs almost nothing to correct.

Where an imbalance comes from

Outdoors, water is rarely spread evenly around a recorder. The Zoom H5 stands on a stone or on the grass a short distance back from the stream. If more of the flow is on one side, that side records louder. The ear on the spot adjusts to this without noticing; a measurement does not. That is why we measure every take instead of trusting what it sounded like by the water (how we record).

Balance is not the same as width

Correlation tells a different story from balance. A correlation of +1 means both channels are identical, which sounds like a single point in the middle of your head. Near 0 means the two channels share little. The brook measured +0.59: the channels have plenty in common, but each still carries its own detail of the water, so it sounds wide rather than flat.

That is why we fixed the brook with a pan filter that only evens out the levels. Collapsing it to mono would also have removed the lean, but it would have thrown away the width that makes a field recording sound like a place. For comparison, the raw window rain we recorded at night in Almaty measured +0.50 by the same Pearson method (rain notes).

How the brook was prepared

  • Trim the spoken slate: we kept 0:20 to 19:55 of the take.
  • Balance the channels with a pan filter to remove the 3.5 dB lean to the left.
  • Soften the top end with a high shelf of −5 dB at 6 kHz.
  • No compressor. The brook is already steady.
  • Normalise the peak to −3 dBFS.
  • Loop to the full track length.

The Mountain River needed far less: its channels were already within half a decibel of each other (−0.46 dB), and a small correction was enough (river notes). Both recordings, with every other measure, are in our measurement data.

A quick check for any sleep track

If a track seems to sit to one side in your headphones, swap the left and right earpieces. If the sound moves to the other side, the lean is in the recording. If it stays on the same side, the cause is outside the recording, for example a balance setting on the phone or the headphones themselves. Choosing between a speaker and headphones in general is covered in how to use a masking sound at night.
