How we record water sounds: our field setup

October 2026 · 5 min read

Short answer: we record water on a Zoom H5 as WAV 48 kHz / 24-bit, always with a fur windscreen, with the recorder on a stone or on the grass a short distance back from the water. At the Kapchagay reservoir it stood right in the shallow water. Each take runs about 20 minutes or more, on quiet weekdays, and every take is measured before it becomes a track.

This page is the practical side of our field notes from the Tian Shan: what we carry, where the recorder goes, and why. I'm Andrey, a field recordist from Almaty, and these are my own setups and photos.

What we carry

AudioZoom H5 handheld recorder
FormatWAV · 48 kHz · 24-bit
WindFur windscreen, on every take
VideoiPhone 16 Pro, 4K, filmed separately
SupportsFull-size tripod with a phone holder; small flexible tripod
Take lengthAbout 20 min or more

Sound and picture are recorded on separate devices. The phone films for our visual channel; the sound in our tracks always comes from the Zoom H5.

Choosing the spot

Most of the work happens before the recorder is switched on.

  • Weekdays only. Trails and beaches are empty, so there are no voices or footsteps nearby.
  • Far from roads. We walk far enough that engine noise disappears completely.
  • Even, foamy water. Water that breaks into fine foam gives a steady hiss. Single splashes or a stone knocking in the current add sudden sounds we do not want.

Places we have recorded this way include the Big Almaty gorge at 1,466 m (autumn 2026), the Alma-Arasan gorge, a mossy pipe spring on a Tian Shan riverbank, and the Kapchagay reservoir shore.

Where the recorder goes

The recorder sits on a stone or on the grass a short distance back from the water. Right at the edge it hears every individual splash. A little further back, the splashes blend into one even layer, which is what a sleep or masking track needs. The phone stands beside it on the full-size tripod.

In the water at Kapchagay

Lake waves are the exception. At the Kapchagay reservoir, near Konaev in Almaty Region, in spring 2026, we put the Zoom H5 on the small flexible tripod and stood it right in the shallow water, so it recorded the small waves lapping around it.

Long takes, a slate, and walking away

Each take runs about 20 minutes or more. A long take means a long track needs fewer loop repeats, so there are fewer seams to hear.

Every take starts with a short spoken slate, which we trim off later. Then I walk away from the recorder for the rest of the take, so my footsteps do not end up in the recording. That habit is also how we found one of our favourite sources, the old pipe spring described in our field notes.

Recording at a good level

We set the level high enough that the water sits far above the recorder's own hiss, and low enough that nothing clips. Our Mountain River take is a good example: it peaked at −6.2 dBFS with an average (RMS) level of −30.3 dBFS, so the final track needed only +2.1 dB of gain. A quieter recording would need more gain, and every decibel of gain also lifts the background hiss.

Small sources up close

Some sources are tiny. At the mossy pipe spring, water drips from an old steel pipe into the moss, and we set up much closer: the phone on the small flexible tripod for macro video, and the Zoom H5 in the moss or grass beside the drips.

Rain and waterfalls

The same setup goes out in other conditions too. Two short clips: recording rain in the mountains, and a visit to the Maiden's Tears (Devichi Slyozy) waterfall in the Alma-Arasan gorge, where water slides down the rock in many thin streams.

After the take: we measure it

A spot that looks perfect can sound uneven, and the opposite happens too. Our Mountain Brook spot looked like a splashy cascade in photos and was almost rejected; the rocks break the flow into even foam, and it became our steadiest recording, with 0.31 dB of loudness variation. So we never judge by a photo. Every take is measured for steadiness, sudden peaks and tone drift, and the results are in one table on our nature sound measurement data page. Why steadiness matters is explained in our tinnitus masking guide.

No sound is generated or added at any step. Even our noise tracks are shaped from these water recordings.
