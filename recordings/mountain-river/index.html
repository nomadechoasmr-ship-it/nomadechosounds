Mountain River: recording notes and measurements

October 2026 · 4 min read

Mountain River is a loud river that stays the same: over 20 measured minutes its loudness varied by only 0.62 dB and it never produced a peak more than 10 dB above its own level. It was recorded in 2026 in the Tian Shan mountains near Almaty. This page shows the numbers, how it compares with our quieter brook, and the short list of things we did to it before it became a long track.

Noisy, but constant

A river is louder and rougher than a small brook. You hear more weight in the water and more texture. For a sleep or masking track that is fine, as long as the sound does not change. What matters is not how loud a river is, but whether it surprises you: a sudden splash, a stone knocking, the level slowly creeping up over an hour.

This river passed on all of those counts. We measured the middle 20 minutes of the take, from minute 4 to minute 24, so that the start and end of the session would not hide anything.

Loudness variation is the standard deviation of the level in 400 ms windows. A sudden peak is a window more than 6 dB (or 10 dB) above the median level, shown as a share of all windows. Tone centre is the spectral centroid.

River vs brook

Our Mountain Brook is the steadiest recording we have, and the river is a little less even. Side by side, measured the same way:

Both are far steadier than birdsong, which measured 3.73 dB of loudness variation with 42 loud events per minute (full birdsong analysis). The river's tone centre sits slightly higher, so its rush is a little brighter than the brook's softer hiss. The river also needed far less repair: its channels were already within half a decibel of each other, while the brook leaned 3.5 dB to the left. Why steadiness matters for masking is explained in our tinnitus masking guide.

A good recording level

The loudest moment in the take reached −6.2 dBFS, and the average level sat at −30.3 dBFS. That means the recorder was set high enough that the river is far above the Zoom H5's own hiss, and low enough that nothing clipped. Because of that, the final track only needed +2.1 dB of gain. A quiet recording would have needed much more, and every decibel of gain also lifts the recorder's background noise.

How we prepared the track

  • Trim the spoken slate and the end: we kept 0:30 to 26:00 of the take.
  • Balance the channels with a small correction for the −0.46 dB difference.
  • Soften the top end with a high shelf of −5 dB at 6 kHz.
  • No compressor. The river is already constant, and compression would only lift background noise.
  • Normalise with +2.1 dB of gain.
  • Loop to the full track length. A long take means fewer seams: a full-night track repeats this section about 18 times.

No sound was generated or added. What you hear is the river, trimmed, balanced and slightly softened. How we pick spots like this one is in our field notes from the Tian Shan.
